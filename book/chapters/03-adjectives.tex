\gpart{III}{Adjectives}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\gsec{Which ending pattern?}

\gintro{An adjective only takes an ending when it stands directly in front of a noun. Which of three patterns applies depends on what, if anything, precedes it.}

An adjective only takes an ending when it stands \textbf{in front of a noun}.
Which of the three patterns applies depends on what comes before the adjective.

\begin{gtbl}{colspec={X[0.85,l] X[1.4,l] X[1.35,l]}}
  Pattern & Comes after & Example \\
  Weak   & a der-word \eng{der, dieser, jeder, welcher, alle} & der \textbf{rote} Wagen \\
  Mixed  & an ein-word \eng{ein, kein, mein, ihr, unser} & ein \textbf{roter} Wagen \\
  Strong & nothing at all \eng{no determiner} & \textbf{roter} Wagen \\
\end{gtbl}

\tcap{The logic behind it}

\begin{gnote}
German insists that case and gender are marked \textbf{exactly once} in the noun
phrase. If the determiner already shows it (\textit{der}, \textit{dem}), the
adjective can relax and take a weak \textit{-e} or \textit{-en}. If there is no
determiner, the adjective has to do the job itself and borrows the der-word
endings. Mixed is the in-between case: \textit{ein} is blank in three slots, so
the adjective steps in there and stays weak everywhere else.
\end{gnote}

\tcap{No ending needed}

\begin{flattbl}{colspec={X[1,l] X[2.1,l]}}
  Predicative   & Der Wagen ist \textbf{rot}. \eng{after sein / werden / bleiben} \\
  Adverbial     & Er fährt \textbf{schnell}. \\
  After numbers & \textit{zwei}, \textit{drei} etc. do not count as determiners \\
\end{flattbl}

\tcap{Stem changes before an ending}

\begin{flattbl}{colspec={X[0.85,l] X[0.9,l] X[1.5,l]}}
  hoch   & $\rightarrow$ hoh-   & ein \textbf{hoher} Berg \\
  dunkel & $\rightarrow$ dunkl- & ein \textbf{dunkler} Raum \\
  teuer  & $\rightarrow$ teur-  & ein \textbf{teures} Auto \\
  sauer  & $\rightarrow$ saur-  & \textbf{saure} Milch \\
\end{flattbl}

\gsec{Weak endings — after a der-word}

\gintro{After a der-word the determiner has already shown the case and gender, so the adjective carries only a minimal ending.}

der, die, das \textbullet{} dieser \textbullet{} jener \textbullet{} jeder
\textbullet{} welcher \textbullet{} mancher \textbullet{} solcher \textbullet{} alle

\begin{gendertbl}{}
  Weak & maskulin & feminin & neutrum & Plural \\
  Nominativ & \en{-e}  & \en{-e}  & \en{-e}  & \en{-en} \\
  Akkusativ & \en{-en} & \en{-e}  & \en{-e}  & \en{-en} \\
  Dativ     & \en{-en} & \en{-en} & \en{-en} & \en{-en} \\
  Genitiv   & \en{-en} & \en{-en} & \en{-en} & \en{-en} \\
\end{gendertbl}

\begin{gnote}
Only two endings exist here, \textit{-e} and \textit{-en}, and the \textit{-e}
sits in a neat block of five cells in the top-left corner. Everything else is
\textit{-en}. This is the easiest of the three patterns by a distance.
\end{gnote}

\tcap{Filled in with \textit{gut}}

\begin{gendertbl}{}
  Weak & maskulin & feminin & neutrum & Plural \\
  Nominativ & der gute Mann    & die gute Frau  & das gute Kind    & die guten Leute \\
  Akkusativ & den guten Mann   & die gute Frau  & das gute Kind    & die guten Leute \\
  Dativ     & dem guten Mann   & der guten Frau & dem guten Kind   & den guten Leuten \\
  Genitiv   & des guten Mannes & der guten Frau & des guten Kindes & der guten Leute \\
\end{gendertbl}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\gsec{Mixed endings — after an ein-word}

\gintro{\textit{ein} and its relatives have no ending in three of their slots. In those three the adjective supplies the missing information; elsewhere it behaves weakly.}

ein \textbullet{} kein \textbullet{} mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer, Ihr

\begin{gendertbl}{}
  Mixed & maskulin & feminin & neutrum & Plural \\
  Nominativ & \en{-er} & \en{-e}  & \en{-es} & \en{-en} \\
  Akkusativ & \en{-en} & \en{-e}  & \en{-es} & \en{-en} \\
  Dativ     & \en{-en} & \en{-en} & \en{-en} & \en{-en} \\
  Genitiv   & \en{-en} & \en{-en} & \en{-en} & \en{-en} \\
\end{gendertbl}

\begin{gnote}
Identical to the weak table except for \textbf{three cells}: masculine
nominative \textit{-er}, and neuter nominative and accusative \textit{-es}.
Those are exactly the three slots where \textit{ein} itself has no ending, so
the adjective supplies the missing information.
\end{gnote}

\tcap{Filled in with \textit{gut}}

\begin{gendertbl}{}
  Mixed & maskulin & feminin & neutrum & Plural \\
  Nominativ & ein guter Mann     & eine gute Frau   & ein gutes Kind     & keine guten Leute \\
  Akkusativ & einen guten Mann   & eine gute Frau   & ein gutes Kind     & keine guten Leute \\
  Dativ     & einem guten Mann   & einer guten Frau & einem guten Kind   & keinen guten Leuten \\
  Genitiv   & eines guten Mannes & einer guten Frau & eines guten Kindes & keiner guten Leute \\
\end{gendertbl}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\gsec{Strong endings — no determiner}

\gintro{With no determiner present the adjective is the only word able to signal case and gender, so it takes over the der-word endings almost unchanged.}

Used when nothing precedes the adjective, and after \textit{viel},
\textit{wenig}, \textit{etwas}, \textit{mehr} and bare numbers.

\begin{gendertbl}{}
  Strong & maskulin & feminin & neutrum & Plural \\
  Nominativ & \en{-er} & \en{-e}  & \en{-es} & \en{-e}  \\
  Akkusativ & \en{-en} & \en{-e}  & \en{-es} & \en{-e}  \\
  Dativ     & \en{-em} & \en{-er} & \en{-em} & \en{-en} \\
  Genitiv   & \en{-en} & \en{-er} & \en{-en} & \en{-er} \\
\end{gendertbl}

\begin{gnote}
These are the definite-article endings with the \textit{d-} chopped off:
\textit{d\/-er} $\rightarrow$ \textit{-er}, \textit{d\/-em} $\rightarrow$
\textit{-em}. The only exceptions are masculine and neuter genitive, which take
\textit{-en} rather than \textit{-es}, because the noun already carries the
\textit{-s}.
\end{gnote}

\tcap{Filled in with \textit{gut}}

\begin{gendertbl}{}
  Strong & maskulin & feminin & neutrum & Plural \\
  Nominativ & guter Wein   & gute Milch  & gutes Bier   & gute Weine \\
  Akkusativ & guten Wein   & gute Milch  & gutes Bier   & gute Weine \\
  Dativ     & gutem Wein   & guter Milch & gutem Bier   & guten Weinen \\
  Genitiv   & guten Weines & guter Milch & guten Bieres & guter Weine \\
\end{gendertbl}

\gsec{Adjectival nouns and participles}

\gintro{German turns adjectives and participles into nouns freely. They are capitalised but keep the adjective endings they would otherwise have had.}

\tcap{Adjectives used as nouns}

Capitalise the adjective and keep the ending it would have had as an adjective.

\begin{flattbl}{colspec={X[1,l] X[1,l] X[1.3,l]}}
  der Deutsche  & ein Deutscher & the German \eng{m} \\
  die Deutsche  & eine Deutsche & the German \eng{f} \\
  die Deutschen & Deutsche      & the Germans \\
  der Beamte    & ein Beamter   & the official \\
  der Bekannte  & ein Bekannter & the acquaintance \\
\end{flattbl}

\begin{gnote}
After \textit{etwas}, \textit{nichts}, \textit{viel}, \textit{wenig} the
adjectival noun is neuter and strong: \textit{etwas Neu\textbf{es}},
\textit{nichts Gut\textbf{es}}, \textit{viel Interessant\textbf{es}}.
\end{gnote}

\tcap{Participles as adjectives}

\begin{flattbl}{colspec={X[0.9,l] X[1,l] X[1.5,l]}}
  Present & Infinitiv + d & das \textbf{lachende} Kind \eng{laughing} \\
  Past    & Partizip II   & das \textbf{gekochte} Ei \eng{boiled} \\
\end{flattbl}

\tcap{Ordinal numbers}

\begin{flattbl}{colspec={X[0.85,l] X[2.15,l]}}
  1st--19th & number + \textbf{-t} + ending: \textit{der zwei\textbf{te}}, \textit{das fünf\textbf{te}} \\
  20th up   & number + \textbf{-st} + ending: \textit{der zwanzig\textbf{ste}} \\
  Irregular & \textit{erste}, \textit{dritte}, \textit{siebte}, \textit{achte} \\
\end{flattbl}
